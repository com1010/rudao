Chapter 5.2

Japan: The Analects and the Abacus#

日本儒商 論語と算盤

商

In 1916, a Japanese banker published a book with a title that sounded like a contradiction: The Analects and the Abacus (論語と算盤). The Analects is the Confucian scripture of virtue; the abacus is the instrument of calculation. His argument was that they belong in the same pair of hands, and that a merchant who drops either one is not half a merchant but a failed one. That book, by Shibusawa Eiichi (渋沢栄一, 1840--1931), is the clearest statement any East Asian businessman has made of what this book calls RuShang: morality and profit are not two systems that must be balanced. They are one system, seen from two angles.

Japan matters here because it industrialized a full generation before the rest of East Asia, and it did so with men who read the classics. Between the Meiji Restoration and the postwar miracle, three figures -- Shibusawa, Matsushita Konosuke, and Inamori Kazuo -- built the practice that Chinese theory had only described. Their moral sources were not identical: Shibusawa was formed by Confucianism, Inamori by Buddhism and Confucian ethics together, Matsushita by a practical humanism that borrowed from both. But all three arrived at the same operating rule, and it is the rule this chapter is about: the firm is not a machine for extracting value from society; it is an institution through which society learns to trust itself.

5.2.1 Shibusawa Eiichi (渋沢栄一): One Hand on the Abacus, One on the Analects

Shibusawa was born into a farming family that also ran a small indigo business, and his first education was in the Analects, read aloud at the village school. He lived through the last years of the Tokugawa order, briefly served the shogunate, and in 1867 travelled to Europe for the Paris Exposition -- where he saw something no Chinese scholar of his generation had seen: joint-stock companies, banks, and railways built by ordinary citizens pooling capital. He came back convinced that Japan's weakness was not moral but institutional, and that the missing institution was the modern company.

What he built afterward is almost absurd in scale: he helped found or foster several hundred enterprises, including the First National Bank (第一国立銀行) in 1873, and he is now honored on the Japanese 10,000-yen note. Behind all of it was a single principle, gappon shugi (合本主義), the joint-stock principle -- capital pooled not for one family's enrichment but for public benefit. Business, in his vocabulary, was jitsugyō (実業), "real business," as opposed to speculation: the difference was not the industry but whether the activity created something society could use.

His best-known formulation of the ethic is shikon shōsai (士魂商才): the soul of a scholar-warrior, the talent of a merchant. Virtue without commercial competence produces a man who cannot pay his own debts; competence without virtue produces a man whose success is a social cost. The abacus without the Analects is greed with good bookkeeping. The Analects without the abacus is a sermon, not a livelihood.

5.2.2 Inamori Kazuo (稲盛和夫): Revere Heaven, Love People

Inamori founded Kyocera (京都セラミックス) in 1959 with a handful of employees and no capital worth the name, added the second national carrier KDDI (1984), and then, at seventy-eight, took on the hardest assignment in Japanese business: the revival of Japan Airlines (JAL), which had entered bankruptcy in 2010.

His company motto is keiten aijin (敬天愛人) -- revere heaven, love people -- a phrase he took from Saigō Takamori and made into an operating principle. "Revere heaven" means accept that there is a limit to what your own calculation can know; "love people" means the purpose of the enterprise is the welfare of the people inside it, not the size of the balance sheet. He reduced the purpose of management to one sentence: to enable each employee to grow as a human being, and to let the company be the place where that growth happens.

The mechanism he invented for this is amoeba management (アメーバ経嬶): the company is divided into small units, each with its own transparent accounts, each led by someone who can see the consequences of his own decisions. It looks like a profit-center system, and it is one -- but the accounting is not there to rank people. It is there to teach them, the way a mirror teaches. He also gave the world a formula he used all his life: the outcome of a life equals mindset times passion times ability (思维方式 x 热情 x 能力). Ability and passion are finite; mindset can be negative -- which is why a brilliant, hard-working person with a distorted mindset produces the largest disasters.

At JAL he took no salary, brought no turnaround playbook, and did the one thing he knew: he went to the front line, talked to ground staff and cabin crew, and rebuilt the philosophy before he rebuilt the finances. The airline returned to profit within two years and relisted in 2012. He did not rescue it with an abacus. He rescued it with the Analects, and the abacus followed.

5.2.3 Matsushita Konosuke (松下幸之助): The Tap-Water Philosophy

Matsushita started in 1918 in a rented Osaka house with three employees, making an improved attachment plug. Out of that came Panasonic (松下電器), and out of Panasonic came a philosophy of production that is still the most radical thing said about consumer goods.

He called it suidō tetsugaku (水道哲学), the tap-water philosophy: manufactured goods should be like tap water -- abundant, reliable, and cheap enough that no one is excluded from them. A product that only the rich can afford is, in his terms, a failure of production, not a triumph of marketing. This is why he set prices low and volumes high long before "market penetration" became a business-school term, and why he wrote a 250-year plan for the company, of which he expected to live only the first few decades.

His second principle is monozukuri wa hito-zukuri (造物先造人): making things begins with making people. Before you build the product, build the person who will build it. He meant it concretely -- in-house education, daily morning meetings where ordinary workers spoke about their work, and later the Matsushita Institute of Government and Management (松下政経塾), founded in 1979 to train people for public life rather than for the company. A company, he said, is a place where people are formed; the products are the residue of that formation.

5.2.4 What the Three Share

Three men, three eras, three vocabularies -- Confucian, Buddhist, humanist -- and one operating rule.

First, none of them separated morality from profit. Shibusawa's 道徳経済合一 (unity of morality and economy) is not a compromise between the two; it is the claim that a business which damages the society it depends on is not profitable but merely solvent for a while. Second, all three treated the firm as a school. Kyocera's amoeba accounts, Panasonic's morning meetings, Shibusawa's insistence that his managers study the Analects: each is an educational device disguised as a management tool. Third, each of them thought in public time rather than quarterly time -- a 250-year plan, a bank for the nation, an airline restored for the people who fly it.

That is the Japanese proof of this book's argument. RuShang is not a Chinese heirloom. It is an East Asian operating system, and it works wherever someone is willing to hold the abacus and the Analects in the same pair of hands.
