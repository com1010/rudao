Why Co-opetition Beats Zero-Sum in Web3

Published 2026-11-21  |  RuDao

Web3 markets move fast. Token incentives, protocol forks, and liquidity wars make competition look like the only rational strategy. Yet the teams that last are usually the ones that can cooperate with competitors without giving up their edge. That is the core idea of co-opetition, and it is the subject of the RuDao book.

Zero-sum thinking is expensive

A zero-sum mindset assumes that every gain by a rival is a loss for you. In practice it produces three predictable costs:

  1. Duplicated infrastructure. Competing teams rebuild the same tools instead of sharing standards.
  2. Fragmented liquidity. Users are forced to choose sides, and the whole market stays smaller.
  3. Eroded trust. Partners hesitate to share roadmaps, which slows down integration and distribution.

None of these costs show up clearly in a quarterly report. They show up as stalled growth two years later.

Co-opetition is not being soft

Co-opetition means competing where you must and cooperating where you can. A protocol can compete on user experience while sharing an open standard with rivals. An accelerator can back multiple teams in the same category while helping them avoid common infrastructure mistakes. The rivalry stays real. The waste is removed.

The Confucian framing in RuDao is useful here. Relationship quality is treated as an asset, not a soft extra. Trust lowers transaction costs. Long-term partnership outranks a single winning quarter. Profit still matters, but it has to be balanced with endurance.

What this looks like in practice

Three habits show up repeatedly in healthy Web3 ecosystems:

  • Shared standards. Agree on the boring layers so talent can focus on the valuable ones.
  • Honest partner maps. Know which competitors you can rely on for distribution and which you cannot.
  • Reputation as collateral. In a small industry, being difficult to work with is a measurable disadvantage.

For founders, the test is simple: does this decision make the ecosystem larger, or only your slice of a static pie? The best strategies do both.

The long view

Markets mature when participants stop trying to eliminate each other and start building rules that make the whole network more trustworthy. That is as true in AI and Web3 as it was in the merchant networks studied in classical Chinese thought.
